What to Do Before a Storm, and When the Pump Quits

A few checks now prevent most sump pump failures.

  • 1
    Test the pump. Pour water into the pit until the float lifts. The pump should start, empty the pit and stop.
  • 2
    Check the discharge. Walk outside during the test. Water should exit well away from the foundation.
  • 3
    Clear the pit. Remove debris that can jam the float or clog the intake.
  • 4
    Watch the check valve. Water rushing back into the pit after the pump stops means a failed check valve.
  • 5
    If it quits in a storm. Unplug and replug it, check the breaker, and free the float. If it still does not run, call right away and move valuables off the basement floor.

A pump is only as good as its discharge. A line that dumps water next to the foundation on a sloped lot sends it straight back to the pit. Extending the discharge downhill and away from the house is often the cheapest improvement available.

Sump Pumps on Roswell's Sloped, Clay-Heavy Lots

Piedmont red clay holds water and drains slowly, so after days of rain the soil around a foundation stays saturated and water keeps finding the pit long after the storm ends. On sloped lots, the uphill side of the house takes the most pressure.

That is why pump sizing depends on inflow, not just the size of the basement. A pit that fills in minutes during a storm needs more capacity than one that barely collects water. A battery backup matters more here than in many places because summer thunderstorms and winter ice both knock out power.

Sump pumps should discharge outside to daylight or an approved drainage point, not into the sanitary sewer. The IPC, which Georgia's plumbing code is based on, prohibits draining storm water into sewers intended for sewage only. If basement water shows up during heavy rain along with gurgling drains, the problem may be a sewer backup rather than groundwater, and main line drain cleaning is the fix.
