Sound Familiar?

The Drain Was Snaked, Then Clogged Again a Few Weeks Later

That pattern is the most common reason people end up needing hydro jetting, even if they have never heard the term. A cable machine pushes through a clog and opens a channel, but whatever is caked on the rest of the pipe wall, whether grease, scale or a mat of roots, stays put. Water flows for a while, the channel closes, and the drain backs up again.

Other signs point the same way: a kitchen sink that clogs every few months, a main line that needs clearing more than once a year, or a cable that keeps coming back wrapped in fine roots. Restaurants see it in grease lines. In every case the fix is cleaning the whole pipe wall rather than poking another hole through the middle.

Roswell 24/7 Plumbing jets kitchen branches, main building drains and sewer laterals across Roswell and nearby towns such as Dunwoody, usually after a camera check shows what is in the pipe.

Two kinds of Roswell lines benefit most: the restaurant grease lines along Canton Street and around Roswell Town Square, and the long residential laterals in East Roswell neighborhoods such as Martin's Landing and Horseshoe Bend, where mature trees put roots into older joints.

How Hydro Jetting Works

Hydro jetting is drain cleaning with high-pressure water instead of a steel cable. A cart or trailer-mounted jetter pumps water through a reinforced hose to a nozzle whose small openings aim mostly backward. Those rear jets pull the hose forward into the line and blast the pipe wall as it goes, sending loosened grease and debris downstream.

Residential jetters typically run somewhere around 1,500 to 4,000 psi, and the plumber chooses the nozzle and pressure for the pipe in front of them. A rotating nozzle handles grease and scale, a root-cutting nozzle handles fine roots, and older or thinner pipe gets lower pressure.

Because jetting reaches the full diameter of the pipe, it is also the standard way to prepare a line for a cured-in-place liner, which needs a clean wall to bond to.

Honest Limits

When a Line Should Not Be Jetted

High-pressure water is powerful, which is the point, but it can break a pipe that is already failing. Cracked clay, badly corroded cast iron and Orangeburg can come apart under jetting pressure. That is why jetting an older Roswell lateral starts with a camera look at the pipe.

If the camera shows the pipe is structurally sound, jetting goes ahead. If it shows serious damage, full cleaning waits until the pipe is repaired or lined, and any temporary clearing is done gently with a cable.

This check matters most in the historic homes near Canton Street and in the older laterals running under big trees in the 1970s and 1980s subdivisions east of GA-400.
