Sound Familiar?

The Water Tastes Earthy Some Months, or Smells Like a Pool

Those are two of the usual reasons people start looking at filters. Neither means the water is unsafe. Both are fixable, and knowing where your water comes from makes the choice much simpler.

About 80 percent of Roswell gets its water from Fulton County, drawn from the Chattahoochee River and treated at the Tom Lowe Atlanta-Fulton County plant in Johns Creek. The rest is served by the Roswell Water Utility, which draws mainly from Big Creek and a groundwater well. Fulton County disinfects with chlorine (sodium hypochlorite), which is where a pool smell usually comes from. Fulton County has also explained that seasonal turnover in the Lake Lanier and Chattahoochee system raises two natural compounds, MIB and geosmin, that give water an earthy taste at tiny concentrations.

Water filtration installation means choosing a filter that targets what you want removed and installing it with a shutoff, a bypass and a realistic change schedule. Roswell 24/7 Plumbing installs filtration systems across Roswell in 30075 and 30076.

Your source water depends on your address. Downtown homes near Barrington Hall and Bulloch Hall are more likely to be on the Roswell Water Utility's Big Creek supply, while East Roswell neighborhoods such as Martin's Landing and Horseshoe Bend are more likely to get Fulton County's Chattahoochee water.

What to Do Before Choosing a Filter

Five minutes of homework prevents buying the wrong system.

  • 1
    Read your provider's report. Fulton County and the City of Roswell both publish annual water quality reports listing what was detected.
  • 2
    Name the problem. Taste, smell, a specific contaminant or concern about lead from old household pipes are different goals with different filters.
  • 3
    Check the certification. Look for the NSF/ANSI standard and the specific contaminant on the product listing.
  • 4
    Plan the space. RO systems need room under the sink and a drain connection. Whole-home filters need a spot near the main.
  • 5
    Budget for cartridges. Replacement filters are an ongoing cost. Factor them in before choosing.

If the house has old galvanized or lead-soldered copper, the pipe may matter more than the source. A repipe fixes the cause.

Questions

Water Filtration Questions

Both of Roswell's water providers publish annual water quality reports showing compliance with state and federal drinking water standards. A filter is usually about taste, odor or a specific concern you have, not about making non-compliant water safe. Read your provider's latest report for the details.

Fulton County has explained that seasonal turnover in the Lake Lanier and Chattahoochee River system can raise levels of two natural compounds, MIB and geosmin, which cause an earthy taste even at very low levels and are not a health risk. The treatment plant uses activated carbon against them, and an activated carbon filter at the tap does the same job at home.

For drinking and cooking water, one under-sink carbon or reverse osmosis unit covers the need at a lower cost. A whole-home filter makes sense when you want treated water at every tap and shower, for example to reduce chlorine smell in showers or to protect fixtures on a well.

Standard activated carbon does not remove much fluoride. Reverse osmosis does. Fulton County adds fluoride as part of treatment, so if removing it matters to you, choose an RO system.

It depends on the filter and your water use. Most under-sink cartridges are changed every six to twelve months, and whole-home filters on a schedule set by the manufacturer. An overdue filter can grow bacteria and stop working, so keep a reminder.

Look for certification to NSF/ANSI standards for the specific contaminant you care about: NSF/ANSI 42 for taste and odor such as chlorine, 53 for health-related contaminants, and 58 for reverse osmosis systems. The certification should name the contaminant, not just the brand.
