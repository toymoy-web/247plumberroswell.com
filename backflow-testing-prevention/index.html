Sound Familiar?

A Letter From the Water Provider Says Your Backflow Test Is Due

For most Roswell homeowners, that letter is the first time they hear the word backflow. It usually concerns the irrigation system. The assembly near the meter or beside the house that nobody has thought about since the sprinklers went in turns out to need a test, and there is a deadline.

Backflow testing and prevention covers the devices that stop water from flowing backward out of your property into the public supply. An irrigation line sitting in fertilized soil, a boiler loop, a pool fill or a commercial process line can all push or siphon contaminated water back if pressure in the main drops. The assembly blocks that. Roswell 24/7 Plumbing tests, repairs and replaces backflow assemblies across Roswell in 30075 and 30076.

Testing is quick when the assembly is accessible and working. Most of the work comes when it fails.

What Fulton County Requires

Fulton County runs a backflow prevention program for its water customers, which covers most of Roswell. According to the County, testable backflow prevention devices have to be tested, and repaired if needed, every year to confirm they work. The test must be done by a certified tester, who submits the results to Fulton County through SwiftComply.

The County also decides which type of device a property needs based on the degree of hazard, from low-hazard residential irrigation up to high-hazard commercial uses, and asks that containment devices sit as close to the service meter as possible. In Georgia, tester certification runs through the Georgia Association of Water Professionals.

If your water comes from the Roswell Water Utility instead, the same principle applies, but check the City's notice for your specific deadline and submission method. The same Fulton rules apply next door in Milton, which is also a Fulton County water city.

An RPZ that dumps water from its relief port is telling you something is wrong, usually a fouled check valve. It needs a repair and retest, not a plug.

The Test

How an Annual Test Goes

  1. 1

    Locate and access

    Find the assembly and open any cover or box.

  2. 2

    Test with a gauge

    Measure check valve and relief performance with a test kit.

  3. 3

    Repair if needed

    Rebuild failed parts, then retest.

  4. 4

    Submit results

    File the report with the water provider.

Water to the protected system is off for a short time during testing. For irrigation, that means no watering during the visit. Remember the watering window too: under the Georgia Water Stewardship Act, outdoor watering for landscapes is limited to between 4 PM and 10 AM.

What to Do Before the Tester Arrives

These steps make the test quick and avoid a second trip.

  • 1
    Find the assembly. Look near the meter and where the irrigation line leaves the house. Note its location for the dispatcher.
  • 2
    Clear the box or cover. Remove mulch, soil or insulation covers so the test cocks are reachable.
  • 3
    Keep the notice handy. The account number and due date on the provider's letter go on the report.
  • 4
    Pause the sprinkler schedule. Water to the irrigation side is off during testing.
  • 5
    Report any leaks. Water dripping from the assembly or its relief port usually means a repair will be needed.

If the assembly split in a freeze, say so when booking. It will need a replacement, not just a test.
